# How this week works

This is a learning challenge for people with a life. Fifteen minutes can count. Missing a day can count too. The point is to build a little firsthand experience, not to perform enthusiasm for a technology.

## What you need

- One AI assistant you can access. Many offer free tiers; you do not need to pay for this challenge.
- A notes app or paper for saved prompts and observations.
- A timer and roughly fifteen minutes a day.
- A healthy amount of skepticism.
- Under 18? Ask a parent or guardian and use the account or supervision they approve.

## Three ground rules

- **Pick one tool for the week.** Switching tools every day turns practice into browsing. You can compare tools later.
- **Keep private data out.** Use invented details for exercises. No government IDs, bank details, medical records, patient information, other people’s messages, or confidential work material.
- **Follow the rules where you learn or work.** A school, employer, or professional body may say no to AI use even when the tool says yes. Those rules come first.

# Day 1: Meet the machine

**Today’s aim:** Build a realistic first impression. Learn what this tool does well, where it gets slippery, and how it wants to be talked to. Fifteen minutes, no perfect prompt required.

## Your 15 minutes

1. **Minute 1–2: Start clean.** Open a new chat. Take a breath. There is no penalty for asking a basic question.
2. **Minute 3–10: Try three questions.** Ask about something you know well, something that needs a current fact, and something that requires a small plan.
3. **Minute 11–15: Look for the seams.** Notice what was generic, confidently vague, or flat-out wrong. Write down one limitation.

```prompt Start with a tour
I’m new to AI. Ask me three questions about what I want help with, then give me one small practice task based on my answers. Keep the language plain. Tell me two things you are not reliable for.
```

> [!WARNING] It is predicting, not knowing
> A language model produces likely language based on patterns. It does not automatically look up a fact, know your situation, or understand consequences. A fluent answer is not evidence.

## You’re done when

- [ ] You have tried three different kinds of question.
- [ ] You can finish this sentence: “This tool is useful for ______, but I need to check ______.”
- [ ] You noticed one moment where it was not a reliable authority.

# Day 2: Upgrade one prompt

**Today’s aim:** Feel the difference between a vague wish and a useful request. This is the single skill that makes everything else in the week easier.

## Your 15 minutes

1. **Minute 1–3: Ask a lazy version.** Try “Help me plan my week.” Notice what comes back.
2. **Minute 4–10: Add what matters.** Give the goal, real constraints, a format, and one boundary.
3. **Minute 11–15: Compare.** Put the two answers side by side. Write down one specific difference.

:::grid-2
```prompt Before: vague
Help me plan my week.
```

```prompt After: useful
Help me plan the next five weekdays around these real tasks:
[paste 4–6 tasks]. I have about [hours available] and want to
protect time for [priority]. Give me a simple table with day,
focus, and a 20-minute minimum version. Don’t fill every hour;
leave white space. Ask me one question if the plan depends on
something missing.
```
:::

## You’re done when

- [ ] You have a before-and-after pair.
- [ ] You can name the details that changed the answer.
- [ ] You have added one prompt to your notes.

# Day 3: Learn something real

**Today’s aim:** Use AI as a tutor, not an answer machine. Pick something you have been meaning to understand — a concept, a process, a piece of software, a chapter of a book.

## Your 15 minutes

1. **Minute 1–2: Pick a topic and say what you already know.** A small useful topic beats a giant vague one.
2. **Minute 3–10: Learn through questions.** Ask the AI to quiz you and wait for your answers.
3. **Minute 11–15: Explain it back without looking.** Then ask for one correction and one next gap.

Need a topic? Try how a bill becomes law, how compound interest works, what an API is, how local AI runs on a computer, or how to read a nutrition label. Choose something you actually want to know.

```prompt Be my patient tutor
Be my plain-English tutor for [topic]. I know [your starting point]. Ask me one question at a time and wait for my answer. Start with a concrete example, not a definition. After five questions, ask me to explain the idea back. Then point out one accurate part and one gap. Don’t praise me unless there is something specific to praise.
```

## You’re done when

- [ ] You have written a three-sentence explanation in your own words.
- [ ] You can name one thing that still feels fuzzy.
- [ ] You have not used AI as a substitute for a teacher, clinician, or required source.

# Day 4: Make something useful

**Today’s aim:** Create one modest, real thing you would actually use. Not a demo. Not a masterpiece. A draft that saves you a little time or starts a stuck task.

## Your 15 minutes

1. **Minute 1–3: Choose a small real task.** An email you have been avoiding, a packing list, a meal plan, a project outline, a plain-language summary of public information, a story idea, a chore schedule.
2. **Minute 4–10: Make the first draft.** Give the AI your facts, audience, and format.
3. **Minute 11–15: Edit by hand.** Change anything that does not sound like you, check the details, and remove what does not belong.

```prompt Make a useful first draft
Help me create [specific thing] for [audience]. The result needs to [outcome].

Here are the facts: [paste non-sensitive notes].
Use this format: [email / checklist / table / outline].
Keep it [plain, warm, direct]. Avoid [thing you don’t want].
After the draft, list three places I should check before using it.
```

> [!WARNING] Follow the rules of the room
> For school or work, use AI only when allowed. If disclosure is required, disclose. Do not let a draft take credit for work, judgment, or facts that are not yours.

## You’re done when

- [ ] You have a usable draft, not just a chat transcript.

# Day 5: Check before you trust

**Today’s aim:** Practice the habit that separates useful assistance from quiet trouble. Check one claim, then do one privacy drill.

## Your 15 minutes

1. **Minute 1–4: Pick a claim** from Day 3 or Day 4 — a date, number, rule, quotation, or “fact.”
2. **Minute 5–10: Ask how it could be verified.** Then verify at least one claim with a primary or official source. If the tool gives links, open them.
3. **Minute 11–15: Practice redaction.** Rewrite one sentence or task so it contains no identifying details. If you cannot remove the detail safely, do not use AI for that task.

```prompt Separate fact from filler
Fact-check this draft: [paste].

Separate verifiable facts from opinions, ideas, and style. For each fact, say what primary or official source could verify it. Mark anything you cannot support as “needs checking.” Do not invent links, statistics, quotations, titles, or dates.
```

## Your privacy drill

Turn real-ish details into placeholders before they go into a prompt.

| Too specific | Better |
| --- | --- |
| A person’s name and address | “A family member,” “my town” |
| A bank, card, or account detail | “Card A,” “a household bill” |
| A patient, client, or student detail | Remove it; ask a general question |
| A confidential project name | “An internal project” |

## You’re done when

- [ ] You verified one claim outside the chat.
- [ ] You found zero invented citations to rely on.
- [ ] You practiced one redaction.
- [ ] You know one question you should ask a human instead.

# Day 6: Build a tiny workflow

**Today’s aim:** Make one recurring task easier without giving up human review. A saved prompt in your notes is a perfectly respectable workflow.

## Your 15 minutes

1. **Minute 1–3: Choose a small recurring task.** Weekly meal planning, meeting prep, a first-pass proofread, a study quiz, a project check-in, or turning notes into a clean outline.
2. **Minute 4–10: Write and test a reusable prompt.** Replace one-time details with clearly marked blanks.
3. **Minute 11–15: Add a human check.** Decide what you will always verify before using the output.

```prompt Turn this into a reusable prompt
Help me turn this recurring task into a reusable prompt: [describe the task]. Ask me up to three questions about the output, constraints, and common mistakes. Then write a fill-in-the-blank prompt I can save. Keep it under 150 words.
```

# Day 7: Teach it back

**Today’s aim:** Turn a week of small experiences into understanding you can keep. Teaching is the test. If you can explain it plainly, you are less likely to be dazzled by it.

## Your 15 minutes

1. **Minute 1–5: Explain the basics to someone** — a friend, a family member, or an empty page. What is AI good at? What is it not reliable for? What is one rule you will keep?
2. **Minute 6–10: Make a mini capstone.** Use AI for one small real task in three steps: define it, draft it, verify it. Save the result and your edits.
3. **Minute 11–15: Write your own policy.** Three sentences. Not a corporate manifesto. Just how you want to use this tool.

```prompt Help me teach it back
Interview me like a patient teacher. Ask me five questions about what I learned regarding [topic or this week’s practice]. Then give me a short report card: one thing I explained well, one gap, and one next thing to learn. Be kind and specific; don’t flatter me.
```

## A fifteen-minute weekly practice

Pick one recurring appointment with yourself. Each week, do one small cycle:

- [ ] Learn one new thing.
- [ ] Make or improve one useful draft.
- [ ] Check one important claim.
- [ ] Save one prompt or lesson in your notes.

That is enough. You do not need to keep up with every release, app, or argument. The useful question is not “What can AI do now?” It is “What is a good use of AI in my actual life this week?”
