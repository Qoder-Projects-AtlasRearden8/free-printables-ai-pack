# Start with the week you actually have

Most planners ask you to invent a perfect week on Sunday and then punish you for being human by Wednesday. This one starts somewhere else: with the time, energy, and support you actually have.

ADHD can make planning feel like a trap. You make a beautiful list, lose interest in it, forget it exists, then find it three weeks later and feel bad. The problem is usually not effort. It is that the plan was built for a brain and a week that do not exist.

> [!NOTE] The only rule
> A plan is a guess, not a promise. If the guess is wrong, change the plan. Do not turn the plan into evidence against yourself.

## Three questions before any list

1. **What is fixed?** Appointments, shifts, school runs, medication routines, bill due dates, and anything another person is counting on.
2. **What is already heavy?** Sleep, pain, caregiving, a difficult project, a social event, a commute. Name it so the plan can carry it.
3. **What would make this week 5% easier?** Not 100% better. Five percent. One ride, one prepared meal, one postponed decision, one honest no.

## The week is not a test

You are allowed to plan for a low-capacity version of yourself. In fact, that is the useful version. If the week goes better than expected, you can add something. If it goes worse, you already have a floor to stand on.

:::grid-3
### Minimum
The smallest version that keeps the important things from becoming urgent. A bill paid. A prescription requested. A reply sent.

### Steady
What you can usually do when nothing is on fire. A little meal planning, a load of laundry, a clear desk.

### Stretch
The thing you would love to do if the stars align. Exercise, a project, a social plan. Optional by design.
:::

# A 20-minute reset ritual

This is not a full cleaning day. It is a short transition between the week you just finished and the one that is about to start. Use a timer, choose a stopping point, and leave the rest.

> [!NOTE] Set the timer first
> Twenty minutes means twenty minutes. When it rings, stop even if you are “on a roll.” The goal is to make the reset repeatable, not to finish the whole house.

## Before the timer

- [ ] One pen and one piece of paper.
- [ ] Your calendar in front of you.
- [ ] A bin bag or recycling bag within reach.
- [ ] The kit: this printable, the one-page reset, and a glass of water.
- [ ] A timer you can hear.

## Minute-by-minute

:::grid-2
### Minutes 0–5 · Empty your head
Write every open loop on scrap paper. Appointments, worry, “I should,” half-finished tasks, that thing you keep forgetting. Do not organise yet. Just get it out of the brain and onto the page.

### Minutes 5–10 · Match the facts
Check the calendar and your messages. Add only the fixed things. Circle the three outcomes that still matter after seeing the real week.

### Minutes 10–15 · Clear one surface
Choose one: kitchen counter, bedside table, entryway, desk. Put trash in the bag. Put items that belong elsewhere in one basket. Do not start a room-by-room overhaul.

### Minutes 15–20 · Set the launch pad
Put the reset page where you will see it. Set out one thing for tomorrow. Choose a loose end you are allowed to leave. Then stop.
:::

## The stop rule

If the timer ends while you are mid-task, write the next physical action and leave it. “Put the receipts in the blue folder” is a better note than “sort paperwork.” A next action is easier to restart than a vague intention.

# Plan around energy, not just time

Two open hours are not the same at 9 a.m. and 9 p.m. Time is on the calendar; capacity is in the body. A plan that ignores energy creates a to-do list that looks reasonable and feels impossible.

## Three kinds of tasks

| Energy | What it feels like | Tasks that might fit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High** | Alert, warm, able to hold several steps | Appointments, paperwork, budgeting, difficult emails, cooking a new meal |
| **Medium** | Tired but functional, needs a clear next step | Laundry, dishes, groceries, routine replies, sorting one pile |
| **Low** | Foggy, sore, touched out, very little margin | Rest, medication you already take as prescribed, one dish, one email, a snack, asking for help |

Your list will be different. The point is to stop assigning high-focus paperwork to your lowest-energy hour.

## Use anchors instead of clock times

Clock times can slide past unnoticed. Anchors attach a task to something that already happens.

- After I make coffee, I open the calendar.
- When I plug in my phone, I check tomorrow’s appointments.
- Before I sit down to watch something, I put one load of laundry in.
- When I get home, I put my keys in the same place.
- After dinner, I take two minutes for the reset note.

## Make time visible

Time blindness is not solved by trying harder. Make the invisible more concrete:

- A visual timer or a kitchen timer.
- A clock within sight of the task.
- A “start at 6:40, stop at 7:00” window written on the page.
- A calendar alarm with a specific action, not just the word “admin.”
- Body doubling: another person in the room, on a call, or on a focus timer with you.

:::panel
### The two-minute entry
If starting feels impossible, make the entry step absurdly small: open the document, put on the shoe, pick up one receipt, write the date. You are not committing to the whole task. You are proving it can start.
:::

## The after-check

At the end of the day, ask two questions. Not “Did I do enough?” That question has no useful answer. Instead:

1. What actually happened?
2. What would make the next hour slightly easier?

# When the week falls apart

It will sometimes. A flare, a sick child, a work crisis, a depression dip, a lost phone, a plan that was too optimistic. A broken week is not a broken system, and it is not a reason to abandon the whole page.

> [!NOTE] The reset does not require a fresh start
> You do not need a new planner, a new month, or a dramatic Monday. You need the next small action. Keep the page with its crossings-out. It is a record, not a failure.

## Triage the backlog

Sort every missed item into one of four moves:

| Move | Ask | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Drop** | Does this actually matter now? | A non-essential organising project |
| **Delay** | Is there a real later date? | A routine appointment that can be rebooked |
| **Delegate** | Can someone else do it, or help? | Groceries, a ride, a two-person task |
| **Shrink** | What is the five-minute version? | One bill instead of the whole pile |

If an item does not fit one of those moves, it is probably not a task. It may be a worry. Put it in a “think about later” box instead of letting it colonise the list.

## The five-minute restart

- [ ] Set a timer for five minutes.
- [ ] Choose one item from the shrink column.
- [ ] Remove one obstacle: open the tab, find the phone number, get the envelope, move the laundry basket.
- [ ] Do only the first step.
- [ ] Stop when the timer ends, even if you feel like continuing.

## Ask for help without a speech

Choose the sentence that fits. Change it so it sounds like you.

- “I’m having a low-capacity day. Could you take over [specific task]?”
- “I missed [thing]. Can we move it to [day/time]?”
- “I don’t need you to fix it. Could you sit with me while I make the call?”
- “I’m overwhelmed. Can you help me choose the first step?”
- “I need a 24-hour extension. Is that possible?”

# Your reset menu

A reset does not have to be twenty minutes. It can be shorter, and it still counts. Choose the version that fits the moment.

:::grid-3
### 5 minutes
Pick one surface. Put away five things. Write tomorrow’s first action on a sticky note. Stop.

### 15 minutes
Do the three-question check-in. Choose one outcome. Put one load of laundry in or clear one surface. Set one alarm.

### 45 minutes
Do the full reset ritual, plan the week’s three outcomes, and make one thing easier for the next day — groceries ordered, clothes laid out, a message sent.
:::

Keep this short. A long review becomes another task to avoid.

1. **What worked?** Even if it was just “I opened the planner twice.”
2. **What got in the way?** Name the obstacle, not your character.
3. **What should be smaller, different, or dropped next week?**

# A week can be mostly Okay

Some weeks have a clean kitchen and three appointments and a lovely plan. Some weeks have clean socks and a phone call. Both are weeks you got through. The planner is not the judge; it is a landing pad.

## The one-page takeaway

- Plan for the energy you have, not the energy you wish you had.
- Choose outcomes, then shrink the first step until it starts.
- Make the plan visible and the entry cheap.
- When the week breaks, triage before you moralise.
- Keep the version that helps; drop the ritual that becomes a chore.
