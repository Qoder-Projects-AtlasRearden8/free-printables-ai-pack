The five parts of a clear prompt

A prompt is just a request with enough context to be useful. You do not need secret phrases. You need to tell the AI what you are trying to do, what it should know, and what a good answer looks like.

The five slots

  1. Goal: Name the outcome. “Help me prepare for a meeting” is clearer than “work stuff.”
  2. Context: Add the who, what, and why. A little real detail beats a long vague story.
  3. Materials: Paste the notes or name the source you want it to use — and tell it not to add outside facts.
  4. Format: Ask for a table, five bullets, a 150-word draft, a checklist, or a step-by-step plan.
  5. Boundaries: Say what to avoid, what not to invent, and what to do when it is unsure.
Prompt recipeThe all-purpose starter
Help me [goal].
Here’s what’s going on: [context].
Use this material: [paste notes or name your source].
Give me: [format and length].
Keep it [tone]. Don’t [boundary].
If anything important is missing, ask me up to three questions before you start.
Prompt recipeWeak: too little to work with
Write about meal planning.
Prompt recipeStronger: enough to help
I need five weeknight dinners for two people who eat vegetarian.
We’re okay with leftovers, but not mushrooms, and we have about
25 minutes to cook. Give me a table of meals, then a shopping list
grouped by grocery section. Ask before assuming we own special tools.

Try this

One line that improves most prompts

“Ask me up to three questions if anything important is missing.” It brings back the missing detail without turning the task into a twenty-message interrogation. Not every prompt needs all five slots — but the brackets are where the usefulness lives.

A good prompt is a conversation starter

You are allowed — encouraged, even — to say “That’s not it. Try again, but shorter and less cheerful.” The first answer is a draft of the request, not a verdict on your idea.

Turn the AI into a tutor

A useful tutor does not simply talk at you. It finds out what you know, makes you produce something, and points to the next small gap. These prompts set that up.

Prompt recipeTutor, don’t tell
Be my tutor for [topic]. Don’t explain everything at once. Ask me one question at a time, starting with something I already know. Wait for my answer. If I’m wrong, give me a hint before the explanation. After five questions, give me a one-page recap.
Prompt recipeQuiz me properly
Make a ten-question quiz on [topic] for someone at [level]. Mix recall, application, and one tricky question. Ask one question at a time and wait. Don’t show the answers until I’m done. Then explain the ones I missed.
Prompt recipeGrade my explanation
I’ll explain [concept] in my own words. Grade it for accuracy and clarity on a 1–5 scale. Name one thing I got right, one gap, and one small next question I should be able to answer. Don’t rewrite it for me yet.

My explanation: [paste]

Use a source, not just a summary

Paste your course notes, cite the book, or name the article. Then add: “If the source doesn’t cover something, say so instead of filling the gap.” AI can explain a topic beautifully and still sand off the one detail that matters. Your source keeps the conversation grounded.

Could you now explain the idea without the chat window open? If not, ask for another round of questions — not another summary. Retrieval beats rereading.

Prompts for authors and small shops

AI can help you see your own work from a reader’s angle. It should not invent credentials, write fake reviews, or promise results you cannot prove. Use it to clarify, not to impersonate.

Prompt recipeReader-first outline
I’m writing a [type of book or guide] for [reader] who is [situation]. They want [outcome], but they’re worried about [fear]. Suggest three possible structures. For each: the promise, the chapter arc, and a strong first chapter. Ask me three questions before you commit to an outline. Avoid generic advice.
Prompt recipeStress-test the blurb
Here is my book description: [paste]. Tell me what a tired reader understands in ten seconds, what’s vague, and what sounds like every other book in this category. Then give me two tightened versions: warm and direct. Don’t invent endorsements, credentials, or sales numbers.
Prompt recipeExplore titles honestly
Brainstorm fifteen titles for [project] about [core idea] for [reader]. Use these tones: [warm, practical, bold]. Flag accidental clichés and anything that overpromises. Then pick three and explain the trade-off in one line each — don’t just tell me what I want to hear.
Prompt recipeA listing that says what it is
Turn these product details into a clear listing for [platform]: [details]. Lead with the outcome for the buyer, include what’s inside, who it’s for, and a plain “what it isn’t” line. Keep my voice. Don’t invent reviews, guarantees, or results.
Prompt recipeTurn real questions into useful content
Here are real questions customers ask me: [paste]. Group them by theme and suggest eight short content pieces that answer them plainly. For each, give a hook, the one useful idea, and a soft next step. No fake urgency and no engagement bait.

Good to know

Your byline comes with your responsibility

AI can make a weak promise sound polished. You are still the one accountable for accuracy, copyright, claims, and tone. Reject anything that makes your reader feel tricked into buying.

Ask for evidence, not applause

AI is very good at sounding confident. A useful assistant can be right and wrong in the same paragraph. These prompts make it show its work, name uncertainty, and separate facts from guesses.

Prompt recipeSource-grounded brief
Using only the source below, make a one-page brief: the main claim, three supporting facts, one contradiction or tension, and what’s missing. Quote short phrases for each fact. If the source doesn’t answer something, write “not stated.”

Source: [paste]
Prompt recipeSteelman the other side
I currently believe [position] about [issue]. Steelman the best case against it, then the best case for it. Label facts versus interpretations. Finish by naming the one piece of evidence that would most change the picture.
Prompt recipeCheck this claim
Here is a claim I want to check: [claim]. Break it into separate factual claims. For each, tell me: (a) what would count as evidence, (b) how I could verify it with an official or primary source, (c) your confidence, and (d) what you might be missing. Do not invent citations or links.
Prompt recipeUncertainty first
Before answering [question], list what you don’t know, what is likely to change, and what could make your answer wrong. Then answer, and keep those uncertainties visible in a note at the end.
Prompt recipeA decision matrix
Help me compare [options]. Ask for my criteria and their weights before scoring if I haven’t given them. Then make a simple table with a confidence note for each score. Separate facts from judgment. Finish with a recommendation and one question I should ask a human expert.

Watch out

A citation is not proof

If an AI gives you links, open them and read them. If it gives you a fact that matters, confirm it with a primary or official source — or with a person who actually knows. A plausible URL can still be wrong, dead, or about something else entirely.

When the answer misses

You do not have to start over. Name what went wrong, keep what worked, and ask for a specific change. Most weak answers improve with one honest follow-up.

What went wrongWhat to say next
Too vague or generic“Give me three specific versions, each with a concrete example and a different trade-off.”
Too long or padded“Cut this by half without losing facts. Put anything you removed in a list below.”
Not in my voice“List the five phrases that sound least like me, propose replacements, then rewrite only those sections.”
Invented details“Mark every factual claim as verified, inferred, or unknown. Rewrite using only verified claims.”
Ignored a limit“Restate the task and my constraints in one sentence. Tell me which constraint you missed.”
Too agreeable“Argue against my idea for one paragraph. Make the strongest honest case, not a straw man.”
Prompt recipeThe clean restart
Let’s restart this cleanly.
What I want: [goal].
What I actually need: [specific output].
The context you were missing: [details].
Keep: [what worked].
Avoid: [what didn’t].
Before answering, summarize the task in one sentence and ask me up to two questions.

Build one you can reuse

Open a note called “My prompts.” Save the version you actually used, not a perfect version you never tried. Add one line about what you would change next time.

Try this

A prompt is not a performance

Short and clear beats long and clever. If you would feel strange reading the prompt out loud to a colleague, make it simpler.

Plain-English field guide · 02

Start with the big picture

AI is an umbrella term, not one product. Most of the tools in today’s headlines are pattern machines: they learn from huge collections of examples, then produce a likely answer. That can be genuinely useful. It can also be wrong with a straight face.

A model is not a brain

Good to know

The most useful mental model

A language model is closer to an extremely well-read autocomplete than to a person with intentions. It predicts likely words, sounds, and patterns from what it has seen. It does not understand consequences the way you do.

The four-step story

  1. Collect examples. Training data is gathered and prepared — text, images, audio, code, or records.
  2. Find patterns. Training changes millions or billions of internal numbers called parameters.
  3. Save the pattern. The result is a model: a learned system that can make predictions.
  4. Use the pattern. When you type a prompt, inference produces an output. The model is being used, not newly educated.

The foundational words

Artificial intelligence (AI)

Computer systems built to do tasks that usually require human judgment, like recognizing an image or drafting text. AI is a broad label, not one thing.

Algorithm

A defined set of steps for solving a problem. A recipe is a useful analogy. AI algorithms can adjust from examples, but they are still procedures.

Machine learning

A way of building software where a system learns patterns from examples instead of following only hand-written rules.

Model

The learned pattern-matcher saved after training. In everyday use, “the model” is the part of a tool that turns your input into a prediction.

Generative AI

AI that creates new text, images, audio, video, or code by predicting patterns from earlier examples. New output is not automatically original or accurate.

Training data

The examples used to teach a model. Quality, gaps, and bias in this data shape what the model can do and where it fails.

Inference

The moment a trained model produces an answer. In a normal chat, inference is use — not permanent learning.

Parameters

The internal numerical settings adjusted during training. More parameters can add capability, but bigger does not automatically mean better.

Chatbot

A conversational interface for a model. It can feel like a person while remaining software that predicts a likely response.

The words inside a chat window

When someone says “the model has a two-million-token context window,” this is the vocabulary they are using. You do not need to memorize it. You need enough to ask a good follow-up question.

Large language model (LLM)

A model trained on large amounts of text to predict what comes next. That simple objective supports drafting, summarizing, explaining, and translating — with mistakes.

Token

A chunk of text, roughly part of a word, that a model processes. Tokens drive context limits, speed, and sometimes the price of an API.

Context window

The amount of text and conversation a model can consider at one time. When it fills up, older material may be dropped or compressed. Think working memory, not a permanent file.

Prompt

The instructions, examples, and context you give the model. A prompt is a request with boundaries, not a magic spell.

System prompt

Instructions set by the app or developer that sit above your message. You usually cannot see or change them, and they can govern tone, rules, and tools.

Temperature

A setting that influences randomness. Lower values tend to be more predictable; higher values can be more varied. It has nothing to do with emotion.

Hallucination

A confident-sounding output that is fabricated or wrong. The model is not lying on purpose; it is generating something plausible. Verify names, numbers, quotes, and links.

Knowledge cutoff

The point after which a model’s training data roughly stops. Without browsing or live data, it may not know recent events or changed rules.

Fine-tuning

Extra training on examples to shape behavior or style. It is not a reliable database, and specialization does not guarantee accuracy.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

The tool finds relevant documents first, then gives them to the model as context. Useful for grounding — but the model can still misread or misuse the source.

Embedding

A numerical representation of meaning. Embeddings help software find text or images that are conceptually similar, powering search and RAG.

Multimodal

Able to work with more than one kind of input or output — text, images, audio, and so on. Check which modes a specific tool actually supports.

Try this

What “memory” usually means

A chat window may remember the current conversation. A product may also store details across chats as a memory feature. Neither is the same as the underlying model permanently learning from you during a normal conversation.

Plain-English field guide · 02

When AI gets tools

A chat model can only produce tokens. An AI product can do much more when it is connected to search, files, calendars, code, or payment systems. The vocabulary here is mostly about permission.

Agent

An AI system given a goal, tools, and permission to take multiple steps — not just answer once. More autonomy means more chances for costly or harmful actions, so check what it can access.

Tool use

When a model can call something outside itself, such as a calculator, search, or calendar. Without a connected tool, it is generating an answer, not checking one.

Function calling

A structured way for a model to request a specific tool action with specific arguments. It helps software connect the model to real operations.

Model Context Protocol (MCP)

An open standard for connecting AI applications to data and tools through a common interface. Think shared adapter, not automatic safety. Each connection still needs review.

API

Application Programming Interface: a way for one piece of software to talk to another. APIs let builders put models inside their own apps and workflows.

Workflow automation

Using software to run a series of steps without manual effort. Automation may use rules, AI, or both; every automatic step deserves a stop button and a way to check results.

Local AI

Running a model on your own device rather than sending requests to a cloud service. It can improve privacy and offline access, but hardware, speed, and quality are limited.

Open weights

A model whose trained parameters can be downloaded and run. “Open weights” does not necessarily mean the training data, code, or license is open — or that the model is unrestricted.

Quantization

A technique for compressing a model to use less memory and run on smaller hardware. It can reduce quality, so compare outputs rather than assuming parity.

VRAM

Memory on a graphics card (GPU). Local models need it to run smoothly; too little can mean slower work or a model that will not fit.

Watch out

The more access, the more verification

An agent that can read your email, change files, or spend money is a different risk from a chat box that can only talk. Start with read-only access, limit the tools, and put a human in front of irreversible actions.

Privacy, safety, and trust

This is not the scary chapter. It is the practical one. You do not need to become a security researcher; you need to know which words describe which risks, and where to look.

Personally identifiable information (PII)

Data that can identify a person directly or in combination: names, addresses, ID numbers, contact details, and sometimes seemingly ordinary details.

Data retention

How long a service stores your chats, files, or account data. Deleting a conversation may not immediately remove copies from backups or logs.

Training opt-out

A setting that stops a service from using your content to improve its models, where offered. It usually does not erase past history; check deletion separately.

Prompt injection

Hidden instructions in content — a web page, document, or email — that try to override what the user or developer intended. It matters most when an AI can browse, open files, or take actions.

Guardrail

A product rule or filter meant to prevent certain harmful outputs or actions. Guardrails reduce risk; they do not eliminate it.

Alignment

The broad effort to make AI systems behave in line with human intentions and values. It is an unsolved research and product problem, not a finished feature.

Jailbreak

A prompt or trick that gets around a model’s safeguards. Treat a jailbroken answer as evidence of a flaw, not as permission or reliable truth.

Deepfake

Synthetic audio, images, or video made to look like a real person or event. Public images and voice recordings can become raw material for deception.

Watermark

A visible or hidden signal suggesting content was made by AI. Watermarks can be removed, missed, or faked, so treat them as clues rather than proof.

Benchmark

A standardized test used to compare AI systems. Scores can help, but they may not reflect your real task, and benchmarks can be gamed or saturated.

AI slop

Low-effort synthetic content produced at scale. It can crowd search results, marketplaces, and social feeds, making source quality harder to judge.

Good to know

“Safety” is a claim to inspect

Ask which harms a product is protecting against, what its testing actually measured, and what a human can review. “Built-in safety” is not a synonym for “safe in every situation.”

Decode the sales pitch

The AI label tells you almost nothing about whether a product is useful, private, or honest. These translations can help you ask better questions than “Is it AI?”

You’ll hearWhat it might actually mean
“AI-powered”Some part uses machine learning or automation. Ask what it does that a simpler tool could not do, and what happens when it is wrong.
“Private and secure”Not a technical guarantee. Look for retention terms, training controls, encryption, and who can access your content.
“Open source”Check the license, weights, code, and data separately. Open weights alone do not make the whole product open source.
“Agentic”It can take actions through tools. Find out which actions, with what permissions, and whether a human approves them.
“Reasoning model”Designed for more deliberate processing. It can still be wrong, and may be slower or more expensive.
“Personalized”Uses your history, memory, or data to shape results. Find out what is stored and how to clear it.
“Hallucination-free”A marketing claim, not a guarantee. No current system is error-free, especially on facts, numbers, and citations.

Three questions for any AI claim

  1. What does it do on a normal Tuesday? Ask for a specific task, not a polished demo.
  2. What data does it need — and keep? Include everything you would be revealing in the input.
  3. How would I catch it being wrong? If there is no way to check, the workflow is too fragile.
Prompt recipeLearn any new AI term
I’m a beginner learning about AI. Explain [term] in plain English with: one analogy, one real-world example, one way it can fail, and one related term I should know. Define any jargon you use. If the definition depends on a specific product, say so.

Fluency is not the goal

You do not need to sound impressive. You need to understand enough to decide when a tool is useful, when it is guessing, and when the answer needs a human with actual responsibility.

Plain-English field guide · 03

Before you paste

Most AI privacy problems do not start with a hacker. They start with a helpful person pasting something private into a box that felt like a conversation. Use this page as a pause button.

Good to know

The billboard test

If it would bother you to see this on a billboard with your name attached, do not paste it into a general consumer AI tool without a real reason, an approved tool, and a plan to redact.

Sort the information first

Usually fine

Public policies, product manuals, your own non-sensitive brainstorming, generic recipes, and anonymized category totals.

Still check copyright and platform terms before republishing what an AI generates.

Slow down

Your contact details, calendar specifics, drafts about identifiable people, unfinished work, personal photos, and location details.

Ask: do I need this exact detail for the task? Use a placeholder if not.

Stop and rethink

Government IDs, financial account numbers, passwords or API keys, patient and client records, private images of other people, and confidential legal or HR documents.

Use an approved tool, a qualified human, or a safer process.

Red is not a permanent “never.” It means this data carries real consequences: do not put it into a general-purpose tool without authorization, minimization, and a clear need.

The four-question pause

  • Need: Is this exact detail necessary for the task?
  • Eyes: Would I be okay if a coworker, client, or family member saw it?
  • Others: Does this include someone else’s private information or story?
  • Permission: Am I allowed to use this tool for this kind of data?

If any answer is no, redact it, replace it with a placeholder, or stop. A slightly less specific prompt is almost always better than a privacy cleanup later.

A real name, phone, or email[client], [family member], [email]
An account or card number[account], [last four only]
A full address or workplace[small town], [large employer]
A confidential project name[new product], [internal project]

The ten-minute account checkup

Menu labels move around. Look for the behavior, not the exact words. Useful sections are often called Privacy, Data controls, Data & privacy, Personalization, Memory, Connected apps, or Security.

Data and history

  • Find the training control. If the service lets you opt out of using chats to improve models, choose that. It usually affects future content, not what is already stored.
  • Review your history. Delete conversations and uploaded files that contain sensitive information. Emptying a chat may not erase backup copies immediately.

Start with the week you actually have

Most planners ask you to invent a perfect week on Sunday and then punish you for being human by Wednesday. This one starts somewhere else: with the time, energy, and support you actually have.

ADHD can make planning feel like a trap. You make a beautiful list, lose interest in it, forget it exists, then find it three weeks later and feel bad. The problem is usually not effort. It is that the plan was built for a brain and a week that do not exist.

Good to know

The only rule

A plan is a guess, not a promise. If the guess is wrong, change the plan. Do not turn the plan into evidence against yourself.

Three questions before any list

  1. What is fixed? Appointments, shifts, school runs, medication routines, bill due dates, and anything another person is counting on.
  2. What is already heavy? Sleep, pain, caregiving, a difficult project, a social event, a commute. Name it so the plan can carry it.
  3. What would make this week 5% easier? Not 100% better. Five percent. One ride, one prepared meal, one postponed decision, one honest no.

You are allowed to plan for a low-capacity version of yourself. In fact, that is the useful version. If the week goes better than expected, you can add something. If it goes worse, you already have a floor to stand on.

Minimum

The smallest version that keeps the important things from becoming urgent. A bill paid. A prescription requested. A reply sent.

Steady

What you can usually do when nothing is on fire. A little meal planning, a load of laundry, a clear desk.

Stretch

A 20-minute reset ritual

This is not a full cleaning day. It is a short transition between the week you just finished and the one that is about to start. Use a timer, choose a stopping point, and leave the rest.

Good to know

Set the timer first

Twenty minutes means twenty minutes. When it rings, stop even if you are “on a roll.” The goal is to make the reset repeatable, not to finish the whole house.

Before the timer

  • One pen and one piece of paper.
  • Your calendar in front of you.
  • A bin bag or recycling bag within reach.
  • The kit: this printable, the one-page reset, and a glass of water.

Minute-by-minute

Minutes 0–5 · Empty your head

Write every open loop on scrap paper. Appointments, worry, “I should,” half-finished tasks, that thing you keep forgetting. Do not organise yet. Just get it out of the brain and onto the page.

Minutes 5–10 · Match the facts

Check the calendar and your messages. Add only the fixed things. Circle the three outcomes that still matter after seeing the real week.

Minutes 10–15 · Clear one surface

The pile is not a character flaw

Paper accumulates because every piece requires a decision: keep it, pay it, sign it, file it, call someone, or throw it away. When executive function is low, “I’ll deal with it later” is a completely understandable storage strategy. The problem is that later becomes a pile, and the pile becomes a source of dread.

The goal of this kit is not a Pinterest-perfect filing system. It is to reduce the number of open decisions in your home. You are allowed to have a messy drawer and a working system at the same time.

Good to know

Sort before you organise

Organising asks “Where should this live?” Triage asks “What is this and what happens next?” Do the second question first. Otherwise you will alphabetise the same unresolved paper three times.

Five principles

  1. One armful at a time. The whole pile is not a task. One armful is a task.
  2. Decide, don’t perfect. A rough category is enough for today.
  3. Write the next action. “Deal with insurance” is not an action. “Call the number on page 2 and ask whether the claim was received” is.
  4. Keep sensitive paper out of sight. Do not leave account numbers, medical papers, or identity documents on the kitchen table when company comes.
  5. Stop when the timer ends. A repeatable 15 minutes beats a heroic four-hour session that leaves you unable to face paper for a month.

  • A pen.
  • A timer.
  • A bin bag and a recycling bag.

You do not need to buy a label maker before you start. A sticky note counts as a label.

Start where you look most often. That might be the kitchen counter, the entryway, or the passenger seat of your car. Pick one. The other piles can wait their turn.

The five-box sort

Label five temporary spots. The names can be rough. The behaviour matters more than the label.

1. Act now

Something with a real consequence in the next few days: a bill, a form with a deadline, an appointment letter, a renewal. Put it where you will see it, not in a drawer.

2. Date it

Something that matters later: a renewal notice, a ticket, a warranty, a seasonal notice. Write the date on the page or in your calendar.

3. File it

Something you may need again but do not need to act on: paid receipts, completed policies, closed accounts, reference letters.

4. Ask / verify

You do not know what it is, whether it is real, or what you are supposed to do. Put it in one place and write the question you need answered.

5. Release it

Obvious junk, duplicate copies, expired invitations, and anything you are genuinely finished with. Recycle the paper; shred what contains sensitive information.

The two-second question

For each item, ask only: What is the next physical action?

  • If the answer is “put it in the action folder,” do that.
  • If the answer is “write a date in my calendar,” do that.
  • If the answer is “I don’t know,” put it in Ask / verify.
  • If the answer is “nothing,” release it.

You do not have to complete the action during triage. Sorting is a separate job from doing. Trying to do both at once is how a 15-minute sort becomes a three-hour research project.

Watch out

Protect sensitive documents

Do not write full account numbers, government ID numbers, passwords, or medical details on this printable. If a document contains those things, move it to a secure place and record only the action you need to take.

The autopay audit

Autopay is a tool, not a personality. It reduces effort and can also hide drift: a subscription renews at a new price, a bill gets charged twice, or a card expires and the payment silently fails. A short audit turns autopilot back into a choice.

Once a month

  • Log in to the payment account directly, not through an email link.
  • Scan the last 30 days for recurring charges.
  • Match each charge to a bill or subscription.
  • Flag anything you do not recognise.
  • Check that the funding source is current.
  • Look for duplicate services doing the same job.
  • Note any amount that changed.

Once a year

  • Recheck insurance, phone, internet, software, storage, and memberships.
  • Look at the renewal price, not only the introductory price.
  • Check whether a free trial will become a paid plan.
  • Confirm the cancellation window and process.
  • Update the card or account if it has changed.

If an autopay fails, deal with the payment first and the feelings second. Banks and providers can charge fees that grow quickly.

  1. Confirm what failed and when.
  2. Make the payment manually if it is safe and possible.
  3. Check whether the provider charges a late fee or reports the missed payment.
  4. Update the expired card or insufficient balance issue.
  5. Turn on an earlier reminder for next month.

If a failed payment leads to a service shutoff or a debt you cannot manage, contact the provider or a qualified local service. Do not use a high-cost “fix” from an unsolicited message.

Every household runs on invisible information: which bin goes out on Tuesday, who has the plumber’s number, how the cat takes his medicine, where the lease is, what to do when the power goes out. Watch out

Keep this private

A household hub can contain addresses, phone numbers, routines, and security details.
